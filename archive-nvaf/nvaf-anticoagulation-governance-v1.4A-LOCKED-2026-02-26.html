NVAF Anticoagulation Risk Assessment Tool

Governance Rationale, Evidence Base, and Intended Use

Associated digital tool: NVAF Anticoagulation Risk Assessment Tool (v1.4A)
Intended setting: NHS secondary care and community practice
Intended users: Clinicians involved in the management of atrial fibrillation

1. Purpose and Scope

This document describes the clinical rationale, evidence base, governance considerations, and intended use of the NVAF Anticoagulation Risk Assessment Tool (v1.4A).

The tool is designed to support clinicians in balancing stroke prevention and bleeding risk when considering oral anticoagulation (OAC) in patients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation (NVAF), particularly in older adults and those with frailty, multimorbidity, or competing risks.

The tool:

  • Uses validated, guideline-endorsed risk scores (CHA2DS2-VASc and ORBIT) without modification.
  • Presents typical annual stroke and bleeding risks side-by-side to support structured clinical judgement.
  • Provides qualitative statements to support shared decision-making rather than automated treatment decisions.
  • Presents outputs using a structured format: Evidence → Extrapolation → Interpretation, plus a short label describing the Basis of recommendation.
  • Explicitly flags when prognosis/goals-of-care may reasonably outweigh threshold-based preventive logic (especially in very severe frailty/terminal illness).
Important: This tool does not replace clinical judgement, specialist advice, shared decision-making, or local/national guidance. It is designed to make clinical reasoning transparent and auditable, not to mandate a treatment decision.

2. Clinical Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a major risk factor for ischaemic stroke, with AF-related strokes associated with higher mortality and greater long-term disability than non-AF strokes. Oral anticoagulation substantially reduces stroke risk in NVAF but carries an inherent risk of bleeding, including major and intracranial haemorrhage.1

Clinical decision-making in AF therefore requires balancing:

  • Absolute stroke risk without anticoagulation, and
  • Absolute bleeding risk with anticoagulation.

This balance is particularly challenging in:

  • Older adults
  • Patients with frailty
  • Those with renal impairment, anaemia, or prior bleeding
  • Patients with competing risks, limited physiological reserve, or limited life expectancy

Guidelines consistently recommend assessing both stroke and bleeding risk, but also emphasise that bleeding risk scores should be used to identify modifiable risk factors, not as sole determinants to withhold anticoagulation.2,3

The NVAF Anticoagulation Risk Assessment Tool was developed to make this reasoning explicit, structured, and auditable, rather than implicit or inconsistent.

Representation of older and frail patients in clinical trials

Although oral anticoagulation is supported by robust evidence demonstrating stroke risk reduction in NVAF, it is important to recognise that older adults and patients with frailty have been under-represented in many landmark anticoagulation trials.

Pivotal randomised controlled trials of warfarin and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) predominantly enrolled relatively fit older adults, with limited inclusion of patients with advanced frailty, multimorbidity, cognitive impairment, high falls risk, or limited life expectancy. As a result, trial populations may not fully reflect the complexity of patients encountered in routine clinical practice, particularly within secondary care and geriatric medicine settings.10,11

Consequently, estimates of absolute stroke reduction and bleeding risk derived from these trials represent population averages, and their direct applicability to frail individuals requires careful clinical interpretation. In frailty, the consequences of both stroke and major bleeding may be amplified, while competing risks and patient priorities may differ substantially from those represented in trial cohorts.

This recognised evidence gap underlines the importance of individualised decision-making, shared discussion with patients and carers, and cautious application of guideline recommendations in older and frail populations. The NVAF tool is designed to support this nuanced judgement rather than to extrapolate trial findings uncritically to all patients.

3. Stroke Risk Assessment: CHA₂DS₂-VASc

The tool uses the CHA2DS2-VASc score to estimate typical annual stroke risk in patients with NVAF who are not anticoagulated.

CHA2DS2-VASc is:

  • Widely validated across diverse populations4
  • Recommended by major international guidelines5,6
  • Simple to apply in routine clinical practice

Stroke risk estimates presented in the tool are derived from large cohort studies and meta-analyses and are expressed as approximate annual percentages, acknowledging population-level variability.7

In keeping with guideline recommendations:

  • CHA2DS2-VASc is used without modification.
  • Sex-specific treatment thresholds are respected.
  • Outputs remain qualitative: the tool does not calculate an individualised probability for a specific patient.

4. Bleeding Risk Assessment: ORBIT

4.1 Rationale for selecting ORBIT

Several bleeding risk scores exist, including HAS-BLED, ATRIA, and ORBIT. The present tool uses ORBIT for the following reasons:

Predictive performance
ORBIT has been shown to perform at least as well as, and in some studies better than, HAS-BLED for predicting major bleeding in anticoagulated AF patients.8

Objective variables
ORBIT relies predominantly on objective clinical variables (age, renal function, haemoglobin/anaemia, prior bleeding, antiplatelet therapy), reducing subjectivity and inter-observer variability.

Simplicity and clinical usability
ORBIT avoids labile INR and alcohol-use variables, making it particularly suitable in the era of DOACs and in settings where warfarin-specific metrics are less relevant.9

Alignment with contemporary practice
ORBIT was developed and validated in cohorts including patients treated with both warfarin and direct oral anticoagulants, making it applicable to modern anticoagulation strategies.8

For these reasons, ORBIT was selected as the bleeding risk estimator in this tool in preference to HAS-BLED, while recognising that no bleeding score perfectly predicts individual outcomes.

4.2 Governance principle

Consistent with guideline advice, ORBIT is used in this tool to:

  • Highlight bleeding risk
  • Identify modifiable factors
  • Inform the clinician–patient discussion

It is not used to mandate withholding anticoagulation in isolation when stroke risk is clearly above the usual treatment threshold.

5. Rationale for Combined Presentation of Stroke and Bleeding Risk

Clinical guidelines consistently recommend assessing both stroke risk and bleeding risk when considering anticoagulation in NVAF. However, in routine practice these risks are often assessed sequentially or in isolation, rather than being considered together in a structured manner.

The NVAF tool presents CHA2DS2-VASc and ORBIT outputs side-by-side, enabling clinicians to directly compare:

  • Typical annual stroke risk without anticoagulation, and
  • Typical annual major bleeding risk with anticoagulation.

Importantly, the tool does not calculate a single numerical “net benefit” score. There is currently no validated, guideline-endorsed method for combining stroke and bleeding risk scores into a single predictive metric for individual patients. Attempts to do so risk creating false precision and obscuring uncertainty.12

Instead, the tool provides qualitative summaries that reflect common clinical reasoning patterns. To minimise reputational and safety risk from the word “uncertain” being misread as tool unreliability, v1.4A uses a short label describing the Basis of recommendation, described below.

Key governance framing in v1.4A:
The tool’s qualitative conclusion is accompanied by a label describing the Basis of recommendation:
  • Guideline-based – threshold logic is the dominant driver.
  • Preference-sensitive – guideline thresholds provide direction but patient values/tolerance of competing risks materially influence the decision.
  • Prognosis-dominant – goals-of-care and time-to-benefit considerations may outweigh threshold-based preventive logic.

6. Design Principles and Safety Features

The NVAF tool was developed using explicit safety-by-design principles appropriate for clinical decision support.

6.1 Use of validated scores without modification

CHA2DS2-VASc and ORBIT are used unchanged, preserving their validated structure, thresholds, and interpretation.

6.2 Structured transparency: Evidence → Extrapolation → Interpretation

The tool outputs are intentionally structured to make the source of each conclusion explicit:

  • Evidence: guideline thresholds and score definitions used (unchanged).
  • Extrapolation: acknowledges that displayed annual risks are typical cohort-based estimates and vary by population/era.
  • Interpretation: clinician-facing synthesis, including frailty context and the need for shared decision-making.

6.3 Basis of recommendation (not a confidence rating)

Basis of recommendation does NOT mean “confidence in the tool”.
It is not a measure of tool reliability and not a rating of the evidence base. It describes what primarily determines the recommendation in that scenario: guideline threshold logic, patient preferences, or prognosis/goals-of-care (time-to-benefit and competing risks).

6.4 Frailty handling: qualitative modifier with explicit safeguards

Frailty is not a validated quantitative modifier of CHA2DS2-VASc or ORBIT. The tool therefore does not adjust numeric risk scores or risk estimates based on CFS. Instead, CFS is used as a contextual layer that changes the interpretation language and the “basis of recommendation” label.

Frailty tiers used operationally in v1.4A:

  • CFS 1–4: no frailty-driven change to the base qualitative conclusion.
  • CFS 5–6: adds “frailty-aware” interpretation (greater emphasis on recovery potential, vulnerability, and priorities).
  • CFS 7: treated as preference-sensitive (high vulnerability but prognosis may still be variable).
  • CFS 8–9: treated as prognosis-dominant (very severe frailty/terminal illness; competing risks and time-to-benefit often dominate).
Safety safeguard in v1.4A (CFS 8–9):
When CFS 8–9 is selected, the tool avoids “positive” headlines such as “favours anticoagulation” or “often positive”. This is deliberate: in very severe frailty/terminal illness, preventive benefit may be smaller in absolute terms, while treatment burden, bleeding consequences, and competing risks may reasonably outweigh threshold-based stroke-prevention logic. Outputs therefore emphasise goals-of-care and time-to-benefit.

6.5 Internal consistency checks

To prevent contradictory inputs:

  • Age ≥75 years is defined once within CHA2DS2-VASc and derived automatically for ORBIT.

This prevents logically inconsistent age band selection across risk scores and reduces user error.

6.6 Reset functionality

A reset function is included to:

  • Clear all inputs between assessments
  • Reduce risk of cross-patient data contamination
  • Support safe use in busy clinical environments

6.7 DOAC context presentation (anchoring mitigation)

The tool includes brief contextual statements about anticoagulation efficacy (trial-derived). To reduce anchoring—particularly in severe frailty where competing risks and time-to-benefit considerations may predominate—the interface reframes this information when:

  • CFS 7 is selected (severe frailty: generalisability caution + feasibility factors).
  • CFS 8–9 is selected (very severe frailty/terminal illness: stronger emphasis that prognosis and time-to-benefit may dominate decisions).

7. Interpretation and Clinical Use

The tool is intended to be used as part of a structured clinical assessment, not as a standalone decision-maker.

Clinicians should:

  • Use the output to inform discussion with patients and carers
  • Consider cognition, falls risk, comorbidity, functional reserve, and life expectancy
  • Address modifiable bleeding risk factors where possible
  • Document the reasoning behind anticoagulation decisions, particularly when the basis is preference-sensitive or prognosis-dominant

The tool may be particularly helpful in:

  • Older adults with intermediate/threshold-zone stroke risk
  • Patients with prior bleeding, anaemia, CKD, or antiplatelet use
  • Situations where clinicians experience equipoise or disagreement

8. Limitations

This tool has several important limitations:

  • Risk estimates are derived from population-level data and do not predict individual outcomes.
  • Frailty is heterogeneous and not fully captured by existing risk scores.
  • Competing risks and non-cardiovascular mortality are not modelled.
  • The tool does not replace specialist input in complex cases.

These limitations are inherent to current evidence and are explicitly acknowledged within the tool’s design.

9. Digital Clinical Safety and Governance Considerations

The NVAF tool is a clinical decision support tool. It does not automate a treatment decision and is not intended to function as a medical device that mandates therapy.

It is intended to:

  • Reduce unwarranted variation by making reasoning explicit
  • Promote transparent documentation and shared decision-making
  • Support governance review and clinician education

Version control is maintained. Substantive changes should be driven by evidence or guideline updates. Changes to framing language may be made for safety and governance clarity, provided the underlying validated risk scores are unchanged and the decision-support intent is preserved.

10. Conclusion

The NVAF Anticoagulation Risk Assessment Tool (v1.4A) supports clinicians in navigating the balance between stroke prevention and bleeding risk, particularly in older adults and patients with frailty where decision-making is often nuanced.

By combining validated, guideline-endorsed risk scores (CHA2DS2-VASc and ORBIT) without modification and presenting outputs side-by-side, the tool promotes structured clinical reasoning while avoiding false precision. Its use of structured output blocks (Evidence–Extrapolation–Interpretation) and a governance-focused “Basis of recommendation” label supports auditability and reduces misinterpretation of “uncertainty” as tool unreliability.

The tool is intended to support, not replace, clinical judgement, shared decision-making, and appropriate documentation. Used within appropriate clinical governance frameworks, it may help reduce unwarranted variation, enhance transparency of decision-making, and improve the quality of clinician–patient discussions regarding anticoagulation in NVAF.
