Purpose
This governance document provides a structured, reference-anchored synthesis underpinning the associated interactive decision-support tool. It explicitly distinguishes: (i) guideline-based recommendations, (ii) evidence-informed operational triggers where trials do not define a single threshold, and (iii) protocol-dependent elements contingent on haemostasis, anaesthetic technique, and local policy.
Important safety boundaries
This framework is not intended for haemodynamically unstable (high-risk) PE requiring immediate resuscitation or reperfusion therapy, for active major bleeding/uncontrolled coagulopathy, or where an alternative emergency pathway is required. In such cases, urgent senior/critical care input is mandatory and decisions must be bespoke.1
  • Scope note: This framework is designed for lower-limb VTE (PE and/or lower-limb DVT) in urgent femoral/hip fracture surgery pathways.
  • Out of scope: pregnancy/postpartum VTE, paediatrics, upper-limb DVT, unusual-site thrombosis (e.g., splanchnic/cerebral), and elective surgery pathways.

This document must not override PE resuscitation/reperfusion pathways if high-risk features emerge peri-operatively; those pathways take precedence.1

Intended users and clinical safety statement (digital decision support)
This framework and its associated interactive tool are intended for use within a consultant-led multidisciplinary team (MDT) decision-making structure (orthopaedics, anaesthesia, peri-operative medicine/geriatrics, and haematology/thrombosis where indicated). It is not designed for unsupervised autonomous decision-making by junior staff or non-medical users.

The tool supports (but does not replace) clinical judgement, local policy, and documented MDT reasoning. It does not issue automated treatment commands. Adopting organisations should assure clinical risk management proportionate to deployment context (including applicability of DCB0129/DCB0160 and NHS DTAC expectations).2–4
How to interpret “time horizons” in this document
Numeric time horizons (e.g., “48–72 hours”) are used as operational escalation and documentation triggers — not as claims of biologically “safe” cut-offs. No guideline provides a single “safe waiting period” after acute VTE for urgent surgery. Where evidence does not mandate a single threshold, statements are written to avoid over-certainty.5,6
Definition: Operational Trigger (Governance Term)
An operational trigger is a senior MDT review point, not a biological safety threshold.
It indicates the moment at which the team must reassess competing risks, document rationale, and confirm the plan, to prevent unintended delay.
It does not imply that surgery becomes safer or that embolic risk changes at that time.

Abstract

The peri-operative management of patients with acute pulmonary embolism (PE) or deep vein thrombosis (DVT) who require urgent femoral/hip fracture surgery presents a major clinical challenge. Hip fracture surgery is time-critical and UK standards recommend surgery on the day of, or the day after, admission, with the NHFD Best Practice Tariff targeting surgery within 36 hours to start of anaesthesia.7,8 Conversely, interruption of therapeutic anticoagulation during the earliest phase of acute VTE introduces a front-loaded hazard of recurrence/progression. This document synthesises guideline recommendations, observational/registry evidence, hip fracture delay evidence, and physiology-based clot-stabilisation concepts. It incorporates multidisciplinary perspectives and makes explicit the evidence boundaries where contemporary guidelines do not provide quantified peri-operative interruption risk estimates. It also acknowledges that for some patients, the primary benefit of surgery may be relief of pain and facilitation of care (palliative intent), rather than restoration of baseline function, particularly in very frail populations.9

1. Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis, remains a leading cause of preventable morbidity and mortality. In urgent orthopaedic trauma—particularly older adults with femoral/hip fractures—the coexistence of acute VTE introduces competing imperatives: the need for timely surgery and the need to minimise interruption of anticoagulation during a front-loaded early hazard period after diagnosis.1,5

Hip fracture care is time-critical. NICE recommends surgery on the day of, or the day after, admission, and to identify/treat correctable comorbidities promptly so surgery is not delayed.7 The NHFD Best Practice Tariff sets surgery within 36 hours to start of anaesthesia as a core quality standard in England.8 Systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrate worse outcomes with delay (including increased mortality and complications), establishing delay as a modifiable harm.10,11

Contextual mortality benchmarks for governance (UK and frail-cohort context)
  • ASA IV operated cohort (NHFD 2015): in-hospital mortality was 16.5% in ASA IV patients (vs 1.8% in ASA I–II).12
  • Frail non-operative trajectory (FRAIL-HIP): 30-day mortality 83% non-operative vs 25% operative; median time to death 7 days non-operative vs 29 days operative. FRAIL-HIP also reports high treatment satisfaction and “quality of dying” ratings in the non-operative group, supporting a palliative-care framing when surgery is not appropriate.9
  • Baseline mortality framing used in UK hip fracture scope documents: NICE scope documents cite approximately 10% mortality within one month and about one third within 12 months (contextual, not phenotype-specific).13

These anchors are included to make competing risks explicit. They do not replace phenotype-specific assessment or MDT judgement.

2. Key governance principles (summary)

Reminder: Time horizons used in this document are operational triggers for senior MDT review and documentation — not biological safety thresholds (see definition above).

  1. Early surgery is the default in hip fracture pathways; acute PE/DVT does not justify routine prolonged delay.7,8,10,11
  2. No biologically “safe waiting period” is defined after acute VTE for urgent surgery; time horizons used here are escalation/documentation triggers, not targets.5,6
  3. Hip fracture surgery is a high bleeding risk procedure; early post-operative management prioritises haemostasis and staged anticoagulation rather than routine full-dose therapy in the first 24–48 hours.6,14
  4. Physiological risk in the context of ASA grading: In frail hip fracture patients, acute PE (including radiologically “small” PE) or a heavy proximal DVT burden may reasonably be viewed as conferring a level of physiological risk comparable to ASA IV, because the disease state can represent an ongoing, unpredictable threat to life in a low-reserve peri-operative context.15
  5. Dominant competing risk in the frailest cohorts: Very frail or high-risk patients who do not reach surgery (or who inadvertently drift into non-operative pathways while awaiting stabilisation for surgery) can have extremely high early mortality. Therefore, “waiting weeks” to pursue theoretical clot adherence may be harmful in selected patients.9,12
  6. Diagnostic limitations and interpretation: In many UK pathways, standard compression ultrasound (CUS) is proximal-only; “no proximal DVT detected” does not exclude isolated distal calf DVT unless whole-leg strategies are used. Tool wording is aligned to this reality.16

3. Definitions and standards

3.1 ASA physical status as a shared risk descriptor

The NHS Data Dictionary defines ASA IV as “a patient with severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life.”15 ASA physical status grades are not procedure-timing rules or treatment mandates. They provide a structured, shared language for describing baseline physiological risk and threat to life in the peri-operative setting.

In hip fracture care, ASA grading is widely used in national audit and outcome reporting and should be interpreted as a contextual risk descriptor rather than a determinant of eligibility for surgery.

For governance clarity:

  • ASA III describes patients with severe systemic disease but without an immediate threat to life. In hip fracture pathways, these patients are generally expected to benefit from timely surgery, and delay is usually harmful.
  • ASA IV describes patients with severe systemic disease representing a constant threat to life. In frail hip fracture patients, acute conditions such as pulmonary embolism, decompensated heart failure, sepsis, or heavy proximal DVT burden may place patients in this category, necessitating explicit multidisciplinary risk balancing rather than routine delay or exclusion from surgery.
  • ASA V describes moribund patients who are not expected to survive without the operation. In hip fracture care, this category often overlaps with end-of-life or comfort-focused pathways. When surgery is undertaken, it is commonly for pain control, facilitation of care, or dignity rather than survival benefit.

This framework does not mandate ASA assignment for any specific diagnosis. ASA grading remains a clinician judgement and is used here to support transparent communication of risk within multidisciplinary decision-making.

3.2 Hip fracture “early surgery” standard

NICE CG124: “Perform surgery on the day of, or the day after, admission.”7 NHFD BPT: surgery within 36 hours to start of anaesthesia.8 These standards are established because delay increases complications and mortality in the overall hip fracture population.10,11

4. Contextual interpretation of early VTE risk estimates

Historical teaching figures for untreated VTE (for example, cumulative recurrence over months and early VTE-related mortality in selected untreated cohorts) were considered as background context. These historical data are not considered incorrect, but such pooled natural-history figures should not be treated as generalisable to modern phenotype-stratified practice or to the peri-operative question in hip fracture care.

Contemporary guidelines (including CHEST 2021) adopt phenotype-specific framing (e.g., isolated distal DVT, selected subsegmental PE, proximal DVT, physiologically significant PE) and do not present pooled untreated-VTE risk numbers as generalisable estimates across all phenotypes.5 This is particularly relevant because the peri-operative decision concerns short, planned interruption with early resumption or staged escalation, not prolonged complete non-treatment.

4.1 Governance question

For governance, the central question is what is the safest and most defensible balance between (i) thrombotic or embolic risk during interruption and (ii) the well-established harms of delaying urgent hip fracture surgery, acknowledging the high baseline mortality in ASA IV operated patients and the extreme mortality observed in frail non-operative trajectories.9,12

5. What contemporary VTE guidelines do and do not state5

5.1 Phenotype-stratified approach (not pooled “untreated VTE” natural history)

The 2021 CHEST guideline on antithrombotic therapy for venous thromboembolism adopts a phenotype-stratified framework, providing graded recommendations across distinct DVT and PE presentations rather than treating VTE as a single homogeneous entity.5

Recommendations are structured according to:

  • anatomical extent (proximal vs distal DVT)
  • clinical severity and risk stratification of PE
  • presence or absence of provoking factors
  • bleeding risk and feasibility of anticoagulation

Within this framework, CHEST explicitly discusses contexts where initial surveillance or no anticoagulation may be reasonable in selected low-risk phenotypes, such as:

  • isolated distal DVT (IDDVT) without high-risk features
  • selected cases of isolated subsegmental PE (SSPE) without proximal DVT or cardiopulmonary compromise

Governance clarification: The CHEST 2021 guideline does not present a pooled or generalisable statement that untreated VTE carries a 15–25% recurrence risk at three months or a 5–10% VTE-related mortality risk in the first month across all untreated patients. No such global estimates are stated or endorsed in the guideline text.5

5.2 Surveillance and no-anticoagulation strategies (IDDVT and selected SSPE)

It is worth noting that CHEST 2021 discusses surveillance or no-anticoagulation approaches for selected patients. These approaches are described in the context of carefully selected low-risk phenotypes and emphasise structured monitoring with predefined escalation criteria.5

In isolated distal DVT, the clinically relevant adverse outcome described is proximal extension, which then triggers initiation of anticoagulation. Early fatal pulmonary embolism is not presented as the dominant quantified outcome in these pathways.

Similarly, for selected cases of isolated subsegmental PE without proximal DVT and without cardiopulmonary compromise, CHEST 2021 permits clinical surveillance, consistent with a low short-term fatality risk in that specific subgroup.

Governance implication: The existence of guideline-supported surveillance strategies for selected VTE phenotypes is incompatible with applying blanket early fatality estimates to “untreated VTE” as a single entity.

5.3 Treated recurrence estimates and their limits

CHEST 2021 affirms that recurrence risk during therapeutic anticoagulation is low in modern practice, supported by contemporary trial-era evidence and DOAC-based strategies.5

However, the guideline does not present a single universal recurrence figure (such as “1–2% at three months”) applicable across all VTE phenotypes and populations. Recurrence risk varies materially by context, including cancer-associated thrombosis, persistent provoking factors, and extent of thrombus.

Interpretive note: Statements regarding low recurrence under treatment are therefore expressed as directional effectiveness rather than absolute guarantees, and numeric estimates are not used as universal constants.

6. Why historical pooled “untreated VTE” figures are not governance-safe for the peri-operative hip fracture problem

Published literature has historically reported cumulative recurrence or extension rates in the range of approximately 15–25% over weeks to months, and early VTE-related mortality in the range of approximately 5–10%, in selected untreated cohorts.23–25 These figures originate largely from historical observational literature and narrative syntheses predating modern imaging, phenotype-based risk stratification, and contemporary peri-operative practice.

Historical sources underpinning commonly quoted “untreated VTE” figures (context only)

The figures commonly quoted in educational and secondary literature derive from multiple historical sources, predominantly from the pre-anticoagulation and pre–CT pulmonary angiography era (1950s–1980s). These include early controlled studies of untreated symptomatic pulmonary embolism (e.g. Barritt & Jordan, 1960;23), post-mortem series demonstrating frequent recurrent emboli and high early mortality (e.g. Sevitt & Gallagher, 1961–1962;24), and later narrative reviews summarising the natural history of untreated proximal deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism (e.g. Kearon et al., 1990s;25). These data informed historical teaching estimates as approximate order-of-magnitude risks, but were never intended as precise actuarial figures and are not presented as pooled, generalisable risk estimates in contemporary phenotype-stratified guidelines.

Such cohorts frequently:

  • pre-date widespread use of CT pulmonary angiography and structured PE risk stratification
  • are enriched for clinically overt or severe disease presentations
  • compare prolonged complete non-treatment with treatment, rather than short, planned peri-operative interruption
  • do not reflect frail orthogeriatric populations in whom competing risks dominate outcomes

Governance boundary statement: The peri-operative question in hip fracture care is not “what is the risk of leaving VTE untreated for three months,” but whether urgent surgery can proceed with a short, planned interruption of anticoagulation with early resumption or staged escalation while balancing bleeding and thrombotic risks. CHEST 2021 does not quantify this peri-operative scenario directly.5

7. Clot stabilisation physiology: multidisciplinary perspectives and governance interpretation

7.1 Time-dependent thrombus organisation (mechanistic/ultrastructural evidence)

Time-dependent thrombus organisation is biologically well established. Following acute venous thrombosis, thrombus structure evolves through progressive fibrin remodelling, inflammatory and cellular ingrowth, and increasing interaction with the vessel wall. Contemporary ultrastructural studies demonstrate that thrombus architecture and thrombus–wall interaction change dynamically over time, with experimentally observed differences across early and later timepoints measured in days to weeks.17

This mechanistic literature supports the intuitive clinical concern that interruption of anticoagulation very early after acute VTE is undesirable where avoidable, as the early phase may be more vulnerable to propagation if thrombin generation is not suppressed. However, these studies do not define a clinically validated “safe day” for urgent surgery, nor do they quantify embolisation risk at specific timepoints under modern peri-operative pathways. As such, biological plausibility should not be interpreted as a timing rule for surgical delay.17

7.2 Governance interpretation (why this does not translate to “wait weeks” in hip fracture pathways)

Hip fracture decision-making requires balancing two time-dependent harms: (i) thrombotic/embolic risk during anticoagulation interruption in the early phase after VTE, and (ii) the well-established harms of delaying hip fracture surgery (pain, immobility complications, delirium, and increased mortality).7,8,10,11

Mechanistic/ultrastructural studies describe how thrombus changes over time but do not incorporate these competing harms and do not provide quantified peri-operative thresholds. In contrast, hip fracture outcome evidence and national standards explicitly prioritise early surgery. NICE recommends surgery on the day of or the day after admission, and NHFD BPT operationalises this as surgery within 36 hours to start of anaesthesia.7,8 Meta-analyses consistently show worse outcomes with delay, including higher mortality and complications when surgery is delayed beyond common thresholds (including 48 hours).10,11

In addition, the severity of baseline risk in very unwell patients makes prolonged delay particularly unsafe. In the NHFD 2015 operated cohort analysis, in-hospital mortality was 16.5% in ASA IV patients, demonstrating the high mortality risk even when surgery occurs.12 In very frail cohorts where surgery does not occur, outcomes can be catastrophic: FRAIL-HIP reported 30-day mortality of 83% under non-operative management, with median survival 7 days.9

Governance conclusion: thrombus organisation over 7–14 days is recognised as biological plausibility, but it does not justify routine delay of urgent hip fracture surgery for weeks. This framework therefore avoids asserting any “safe day” threshold and instead requires explicit multidisciplinary documentation of the trade-off, proceeding with surgery as early as clinically safe using haemostasis-first staged anticoagulation.

8. ASA IV positioning of acute PE and heavy proximal DVT burden in frail hip fracture patients

8.1 ASA physical status definition

The NHS Data Dictionary defines ASA IV as: “A patient with severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life.”15

ASA classification is not a procedural timing tool; it is a structured descriptor of physiological risk and threat-to-life in the peri-operative context.

8.2 Governance rationale for ASA-IV-type framing in acute PE / proximal DVT

In frail hip fracture patients, acute PE (including radiologically “small” PE) or heavy proximal DVT burden may reasonably be treated as ASA-IV-type physiology for decision framing because:

  • the disease state carries an unpredictable risk of sudden deterioration;
  • physiological reserve is limited in older, frail patients;
  • peri-operative anticoagulation interruption may be unavoidable;
  • surgical stress, immobility, and delirium amplify vulnerability;
  • the combined context may meet the ASA IV definition of a constant threat to life.

This framing is consistent with national outcome data demonstrating high baseline mortality in ASA IV hip fracture patients, even when surgery occurs. In an analysis of the UK National Hip Fracture Database, in-hospital mortality in ASA IV patients was 16.5%, compared with 1.8% in ASA I–II patients.12

8.3 Governance clarification

Important note: This document does not mandate that all patients with PE or DVT must be labelled ASA IV. ASA classification remains clinician-specific. The term “ASA-IV-type physiology” is used here as a governance risk-framing construct to support multidisciplinary balancing in frail hip fracture pathways where competing life-threatening risks coexist.

9. Surgical delay and its consequences in femoral/hip fracture pathways

Hip fracture delay is independently associated with worse outcomes. NICE and NHFD embed early surgery as a quality expectation, and meta-analyses demonstrate improved outcomes with earlier surgery and worse outcomes when delayed beyond common thresholds (including 48 hours).7,8,10,11

Audit expectation: delays beyond agreed institutional standards (e.g., 36 hours to start of anaesthesia) should trigger documented senior review and a recorded rationale for delay.7,8

10. Dominant competing risk in very frail cohorts: outcomes when surgery does not occur

10.1 UK outcome context (baseline severity)

Hip fracture is a high-mortality event even with modern care. NICE scope documents cite approximately 10% mortality within one month and about one third within 12 months (contextual, not phenotype-specific).13 In the NHFD 2015 operated cohort analysis, in-hospital mortality in ASA IV patients was 16.5%, indicating the severe baseline risk even when surgery occurs.12

10.2 Non-operative trajectories: mortality and quality-of-life framing

In frail institutionalised older adults with proximal femoral fracture, non-operative management is associated with extremely high short-term mortality. FRAIL-HIP reported 30-day mortality of 83% in the non-operative group vs 25% in the operative group, and median time to death of 7 days vs 29 days, respectively.9

FRAIL-HIP also reports outcomes relevant to palliative framing: treatment satisfaction was high in both groups and proxies/caregivers in the non-operative group frequently rated quality of dying as good to almost perfect, supporting that when surgery is not appropriate, structured comfort-focused pathways can be high-quality care.9

Governance implication: In very frail/high-risk (often ASA-IV-type) patients, prolonged delay can functionally become a non-operative pathway. Therefore “waiting weeks” to pursue theoretical clot adherence may expose patients to an immediate, substantial risk of death and avoidable suffering (pain, immobility complications). Decisions must explicitly consider comfort and quality-of-life goals alongside survival and thromboembolic risk.

11. Timing of surgery after acute PE: “both sides” and final governance position

11.1 Evidence limitations and multidisciplinary concern regarding timing after acute PE

During review, the limited direct evidence base informing surgical timing after acute PE in hip fracture patients was emphasised. The principal peri-operative hip fracture + acute PE timing study frequently cited in this context is a retrospective single-centre analysis (PMID 32274089).22 The study population is selected (predominantly low- or intermediate-risk PE) and the design cannot exclude selection bias or residual confounding. In addition, clinical practice patterns (including IVC filter use) may vary across centres and eras, limiting generalisability.

This concern is accepted: one retrospective single-centre study cannot define a universal “safe” timepoint for surgery after PE. Governance therefore avoids language that implies a fixed timing recommendation.

11.2 What the available hip fracture + PE study can and cannot support

Kim et al. report that early surgery in selected hip fracture patients with acute low-/intermediate-risk PE appeared feasible and did not show an obvious signal of catastrophic thromboembolic deterioration within the limitations of the design.22 However, as a retrospective single-centre study, it cannot establish superiority of early surgery, cannot define an optimal timing threshold, and cannot be extrapolated to high-risk PE physiology. The authors explicitly call for prospective studies.22

11.3 Final governance position (why urgent hip fracture surgery generally cannot wait for weeks)

Recommendation: Hip fracture surgery should proceed as early as clinically safe, aligned to UK hip fracture standards, with multidisciplinary (orthopaedics, anaesthesia, peri-operative medicine/geriatrics, haematology where indicated) agreement on a haemostasis-first staged anticoagulation plan.7,8,10,11

The justification for not waiting “weeks” is not based on claiming a biologically safe clot-age threshold. It is based on the competing, well-established harms of delay and the high baseline mortality in very unwell patients. NICE recommends surgery on the day of or day after admission, NHFD BPT sets 36 hours as a quality standard, and meta-analyses demonstrate worse outcomes when surgery is delayed beyond common thresholds (including 48 hours).7,8,10,11

In addition, the mortality risk in the sickest operated patients is substantial (NHFD analysis: 16.5% in-hospital mortality in ASA IV).12 Prolonged delay risks clinical deterioration, prolonged immobility and complications, and in very frail cohorts may shift the trajectory toward non-operative management, where outcomes can be extremely poor (FRAIL-HIP: 83% 30-day mortality under non-operative management; median survival 7 days).9

48–72 hours is not a target. If the term appears in operational planning, it is used solely as an escalation/documentation trigger: if surgery has not occurred by this point, the MDT should explicitly document the rationale for ongoing delay, the accepted risks, and the mitigation plan. This avoids implying a biologically “safe point” while retaining a practical governance threshold for review and escalation.

12. Diagnostic and risk stratification requirements

12.1 Pulmonary embolism severity

PE severity stratification is central. ESC guidance classifies PE into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories based on haemodynamic status, RV dysfunction, and myocardial injury biomarkers. High-risk PE (shock/hypotension) generally precludes routine urgent surgery until stabilised.1

12.2 DVT status, anatomy, and the “distal DVT” epistemology correction

DVT anatomy is clinically relevant: documented proximal DVT represents an ongoing embolic source and materially alters peri-operative mitigation considerations. Where DVT anatomy is unknown, conservative assumptions are required.

Distal DVT, proximal-only CUS, and governance alignment

In many UK services, standard CUS evaluates proximal veins only. NICE evidence reviews report substantially higher sensitivity for proximal DVT than distal DVT on ultrasound, supporting why pathways often prioritise proximal assessment.16 A finding of “no proximal DVT detected” does not exclude isolated distal calf DVT unless whole-leg ultrasound strategies are used.

Tool alignment (wording, no logic change): the tool’s GREEN DVT wording is “No proximal DVT detected on CUS (distal calf veins not routinely assessed in standard pathways)”. AMBER/RED remain driven by documented proximal anatomy and anticipated interruption/escalation feasibility.

13. Balancing bleeding and thrombotic risk (operational triggers)

Bleeding risk is determined by surgical factors (extent of dissection, anticipated haemostasis), patient factors (coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, anaemia), and concomitant medications. Patients in whom escalation to therapeutic anticoagulation is not expected to be feasible for a prolonged period (commonly >72 hours post-operatively) represent a high-risk scenario requiring escalation and mitigation planning. Time horizons used in the tool (e.g., 48–72 hours) function as operational escalation and documentation triggers, not biological safety thresholds, and prompt multidisciplinary review and recorded justification of the chosen mitigation strategy.13,14

GREEN / AMBER / RED framework: definitions and required mitigations (governance specification)

GREEN category

Definition (lowest relative risk band within this framework):

  • PE: haemodynamically stable low-risk or intermediate-low-risk phenotype (no shock/hypotension; no requirement for reperfusion therapy), with risk stratification completed per ESC prerequisites.1
  • DVT: no proximal DVT detected on standard CUS (distal calf DVT not excluded unless whole-leg strategy used).16
  • Anticoagulation plan: staged post-operative escalation expected to be feasible within a clinically reasonable timeframe, subject to haemostasis and neuraxial constraints.6,14

Required mitigations:

  • Proceed to surgery as early as clinically feasible, aligned to hip fracture standards.7,8
  • Document: PE phenotype/physiology; “no proximal DVT detected” wording; anticoagulation interruption and staged escalation plan.
  • Mechanical prophylaxis and early mobilisation where feasible; re-assess post-operatively for haemostasis and escalation timing.

AMBER category

Definition (heightened thromboembolic risk without immediate life-threatening instability):

  • PE: intermediate-risk phenotype (e.g., RV dysfunction and/or biomarker positivity) but haemodynamically stable.1
  • and/or documented proximal DVT (popliteal or above).
  • Anticoagulation plan: staged escalation likely delayed because of bleeding risk or neuraxial constraints.

Required mitigations:

  • Consultant-level MDT discussion documented (orthopaedics + anaesthesia + peri-operative medicine/geriatrics; haematology where indicated).
  • Explicit plan for monitoring and escalation triggers (HDU/ICU availability, deterioration triggers, imaging triggers).
  • Proceed with surgery as early as clinically safe; avoid delaying solely to convert AMBER to GREEN.

RED category

Definition (very high risk; mitigation required):

  • PE: high-risk PE with shock/hypotension or requiring reperfusion therapy, or deteriorating physiology attributable to PE (out of scope for routine tool use).1
  • and/or extensive proximal/iliofemoral DVT, or anticipated inability to restart therapeutic anticoagulation for a prolonged period (commonly >72 hours post-operatively).

Required mitigations:

  • Mandatory senior MDT decision-making with explicit documentation of the trade-off and a time-defined reassessment plan.
  • Enhanced monitoring plan (often HDU/ICU-level readiness) and staged anticoagulation strategy consistent with haemostasis-first principles.
  • Consideration of exceptional measures (e.g., temporary retrievable IVC filter) only under restrictive criteria and with a retrieval plan (see Section 15).19–21

IVC filters are not first-line mitigation and should only be considered after senior MDT discussion with haematology/thrombosis input, and only where a documented retrieval plan exists.19–21

Governance safeguards: Unknown DVT anatomy should not be treated as “low risk”; mixed-domain risk should default to escalation; and “lowest band” does not mean “no risk”.

14. Peri-operative anticoagulation strategy (governance-focused)

14.1 Procedure bleeding risk and multidisciplinary consensus

Hip fracture surgery is a high bleeding risk procedure in peri-operative antithrombotic taxonomy. Peri-operative guidance emphasises tailoring resumption to procedure bleeding risk and clinical context rather than prescribing universal schedules.6

14.2 Post-operative anticoagulation intensity: why early full-dose therapy is not the default

Multidisciplinary consensus is that full-dose anticoagulation within 24 hours after hip fracture surgery should not be presented as default. This framework supports haemostasis-first staged escalation: mechanical measures and prophylactic/intermediate-dose anticoagulation when haemostasis is secure (and anaesthetic constraints allow), with escalation to full therapeutic dosing typically delayed until approximately 48–72 hours, and earlier escalation only by explicit senior MDT agreement.6,14

14.3 Pre-operative interruption (pragmatic, verifiable guidance)

For therapeutic-dose LMWH, peri-operative guidance commonly uses at least 24 hours between last therapeutic dose and surgery, recognising that renal function, neuraxial technique, and bleeding risk may require longer intervals.6

14.4 Neuraxial/regional anaesthesia constraints

Regional/neuraxial techniques impose timing constraints for antithrombotic drugs; ESAIC/ESRA guidance provides detailed safety recommendations. Neuraxial safety requirements take precedence, and the VTE mitigation plan must be adapted accordingly (e.g. mechanical measures and MDT-agreed interim strategies).18

15. Inferior vena cava (IVC) filters (restrictive evidence-based role)

IVC filters do not treat thrombosis and carry device-related risks. Their contemporary role is narrow and should be exceptional. Where anticoagulation is feasible, routine IVC filter use is not supported by randomised evidence.

In PREPIC2 (retrievable filter plus anticoagulation vs anticoagulation alone in patients with acute PE at high risk of recurrence), adding a retrievable IVC filter did not reduce recurrent symptomatic PE at 3 months compared with anticoagulation alone.19 PREPIC (permanent filter) demonstrated reduced PE but increased DVT and no survival benefit at long-term follow-up, supporting that systematic use in unselected patients is not recommended.20

Regulatory guidance emphasises removal once protection is no longer needed. The FDA safety communication advises clinicians to consider removing retrievable filters as soon as protection from PE is no longer required, to reduce long-term device-related complications.21

Governance-safe indication framing (restrictive): In the peri-operative femoral/hip fracture setting, IVC filters should not be planned routinely. Consideration should be restricted to exceptional circumstances where all of the following are present:

  • Documented proximal DVT (ongoing embolic source), and
  • Therapeutic anticoagulation is not feasible for a clinically meaningful period (commonly >72 hours), due to bleeding/haemostasis constraints, and
  • Clinical context suggests unacceptably high embolic risk during interruption, despite mechanical measures and MDT-agreed staged strategies, and
  • A documented plan exists for prompt retrieval once anticoagulation can be re-established.

Governance safeguards: Filter consideration requires consultant-level MDT documentation of rationale, anticipated duration off anticoagulation, and retrieval responsibility. Filters should not be used as a routine strategy to “enable neuraxial anaesthesia” where anticoagulation can otherwise be resumed in a short timeframe; neuraxial safety constraints should be managed via anaesthetic technique choice and staged anticoagulation planning, rather than routine filter placement.

16. Practical documentation standards (audit-ready)

  1. State phenotype clearly: PE severity (haemodynamics, oxygen requirement, RV assessment, biomarkers), DVT level/anatomy if known.
  2. State goals of care: survival, function, and when relevant explicit comfort-focused goals (pain control, facilitation of care) consistent with palliative intent in frail patients.9
  3. State anticoagulation plan: last dose, expected interruption window, restart feasibility, staged escalation plan, neuraxial constraints.
  4. Record why delay is/ isn’t chosen: specify correctable reversible instability vs time-limited risk-balancing delay vs comfort-focused pathway where surgery is not appropriate.

17. Methods: Uncertainty and evidence handling

This synthesis integrates: ESC PE guidance;1 UK hip fracture standards (NICE CG124; NHFD BPT);7,8 meta-analysis evidence on harms from surgical delay;10,11 CHEST VTE disease guideline framing and phenotype stratification;5 peri-operative antithrombotic guidance and bleeding-risk principles;6 neuraxial/regional safety guidance (ESAIC/ESRA);18 mechanistic ultrastructural evidence on time-dependent thrombus organisation (plausibility, not threshold);17 and cohort evidence on extreme mortality and quality-of-life considerations in frail non-operative trajectories (FRAIL-HIP), alongside UK ASA IV operated mortality anchors from NHFD.9,12
