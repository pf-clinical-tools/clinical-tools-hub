1. Background and Rationale

Urinary catheterisation and trial without catheter (TWOC) are common interventions in patients admitted with femoral and pelvic fractures, particularly in the peri-operative period. However, practice is variable, and inappropriate catheter use is associated with recognised harms including catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI), reduced mobility, delayed rehabilitation, and patient discomfort and distress.1,2

Conversely, delayed recognition of clinically significant urinary retention can result in bladder over-distension, acute kidney injury, hydronephrosis, and prolonged catheter dependence.3

Older and frail trauma patients present particular challenges because symptoms of urinary retention are often unreliable. Pain, immobility, delirium, opioid use, and impaired communication may blunt or distort symptom reporting, while physiological reserve is limited.4,5

This creates a need for structured, conservative, volume-based decision support that can be applied consistently by nursing staff while preserving escalation pathways and clinical judgement.

2. Purpose of the Tool

The purpose of this tool is to support safe, auditable bedside decision-making regarding:

  • when catheterisation is indicated,
  • when catheterisation should be avoided,
  • when TWOC may be attempted, and
  • how TWOC outcomes should be interpreted.

The tool does not replace local policy, senior clinical judgement, or urological advice. It functions as a decision-support aid rather than a prescriptive protocol.

3. Development Methodology

3.1 Evidence sources

The logic and thresholds used in the tool were derived from:

  • UK and international guidance on catheter use, CAUTI prevention, and lower urinary tract assessment,6–9
  • urological and geriatric medicine reviews relevant to urinary retention and frailty,3–5,10
  • observational literature on bladder volume thresholds and post-void residual interpretation,11–13
  • pragmatic standards commonly embedded in NHS nursing standard operating procedures.

No single guideline provides a fully specified algorithm for peri-operative catheter management in femoral and pelvic fracture patients. The tool therefore synthesises convergent practice standards rather than extrapolating from a single source.

3.2 Design philosophy

Bladder volume over subjective symptoms
Objective bladder volume is prioritised because symptoms are frequently unreliable in frail, delirious, or cognitively impaired patients.

Screening for pain and delirium as context, not as a barrier
Pain and delirium may be caused by urinary retention, contribute to retention, or coexist with it. The screening step therefore modifies context but does not block bladder assessment.

Conservative thresholds
Thresholds are chosen to minimise harm from bladder over-distension while avoiding unnecessary catheterisation.

Explicit STOP and escalation rules
Situations where catheterisation should not proceed without senior input are clearly identified.

Auditability
Each output is accompanied by explicit rationale suitable for contemporaneous documentation.

4. Evidence Underpinning the Screening Step

Delirium and pain are common in peri-operative hip-fracture care and may worsen agitation, mobility, continence, and communication.4,5 Urinary retention may contribute to agitation or delirium, but it is not a specific explanation and should be considered alongside other reversible causes.

The tool therefore screens for pain and delirium first in order to provide context and prompt early treatment of reversible contributors. However, this step does not prevent bladder assessment when retention risk exists.

5. Evidence Underpinning Catheterisation Decisions

5.1 Why bladder volume is central

Symptoms of urinary retention are poorly sensitive in older adults. Clinically significant retention may be present without classic discomfort, particularly in patients with delirium, immobility, neurological impairment, or impaired communication.4,11

Bladder scanning provides a non-invasive and reproducible measure and is therefore widely recommended to guide decision-making.6,9

In the current tool, once a bladder scan or post-void residual (PVR) is available, that volume is prioritised over timing since last void. Time since last void is used primarily to decide whether a scan is needed, not to override a known high volume.

5.2 Symptomatic retention (≥300 mL)

A post-void residual or bladder volume of ≥300 mL is commonly cited as indicating inadequate bladder emptying and clinically relevant retention.11,12

The tool therefore recommends catheterisation when signs suggesting retention are present and bladder volume is ≥300 mL. Volumes below this threshold are interpreted more cautiously and prompt reassessment rather than immediate catheterisation.

5.3 Sex-specific asymptomatic bladder volumes

In asymptomatic patients, higher thresholds are appropriate in order to avoid unnecessary catheterisation. Observational literature associates bladder volumes above approximately 500 mL with increasing risk of over-distension and renal sequelae, but does not provide a single universally accepted operational threshold for frail trauma patients.3,13

The current tool therefore applies the following pragmatic sex-specific asymptomatic thresholds:

Operational bladder volume thresholds used in the tool

Clinical context Bladder volume threshold Recommended action
Signs suggesting urinary retention ≥300 mL Catheterisation recommended
Asymptomatic – male ≥500 mL Catheterisation recommended
Asymptomatic – female ≥600 mL Catheterisation recommended

Intermediate volumes are handled conservatively:

  • Male/unknown sex: 300–499 mL → observe and repeat scan.
  • Female: 400–599 mL → observe and repeat scan.

These sex-specific thresholds are pragmatic rather than directly trial-derived. They were chosen to reduce unnecessary catheterisation while still limiting the risk of over-distension in a frail, often poorly communicative population.

5.4 Overriding clinical indications

Certain clinical states justify deviation from standard volume thresholds, including acute kidney injury, hydronephrosis, or rapidly rising bladder volumes. These reflect organ-level risk where delay may result in harm.3,8

The tool therefore allows threshold override only with senior clinical review in these circumstances.

6. Evidence Underpinning TWOC Eligibility

Successful TWOC depends not only on bladder physiology but also on contextual safety factors, including haemodynamic stability, pain control, availability of bladder scanning, and adequate staffing and mobility support.

Guidance emphasises that TWOC should be attempted only when monitoring and re-intervention are feasible.6,9 The eligibility checklist reflects these principles and is designed to prevent unsafe or poorly supported TWOC attempts.

7. Evidence Underpinning TWOC Outcome Interpretation

7.1 Primary role of post-void bladder volume

Post-void residual volume is the most reliable indicator of TWOC success or failure.11–13 The tool treats PVR <200 mL as compatible with successful voiding and PVR ≥300 mL as indicating failed TWOC. Intermediate values imply uncertainty and warrant observation rather than automatic re-catheterisation.

7.2 Role of symptoms

Symptoms such as discomfort or distress increase concern but do not replace objective residual volume. In frail patients, symptoms may be absent despite significant retention or present despite smaller residuals.4,11

The tool therefore uses symptoms to increase urgency and to identify “symptomatic residual”, but not to override a clearly low or clearly high bladder volume.

8. Safety, Risk, and Escalation

The tool embeds safety features including prohibition of catheterisation without senior input in suspected urethral injury or macroscopic haematuria, STOP rules for pain, resistance, distress, or patient instability during catheterisation, and escalation where complications arise.8,9

These rules are intended to reduce iatrogenic harm and should be treated as procedural safety principles rather than discretionary prompts.

9. Limitations and Uncertainty

The evidence base for catheter management in femoral and pelvic fracture patients is limited. Most thresholds derive from mixed medical and surgical populations rather than trauma-specific prospective trials. Accordingly, several thresholds used in the tool are pragmatic rather than definitive, and clinical judgement remains essential.

The tool synthesises guideline recommendations, observational evidence, and pragmatic clinical reasoning. In several areas the literature does not provide precise operational thresholds for peri-operative urinary retention in orthogeriatric trauma patients. Where this occurs, the algorithm applies conservative, pragmatic thresholds designed to minimise patient harm while avoiding unnecessary catheterisation.

The confidence assessments below therefore distinguish between direct evidence, clinical extrapolation, and interpretative judgement, allowing readers to understand how each component of the tool was derived.

Evidence
Delirium and pain are common in hip-fracture care and are associated with poorer outcomes. Guidance supports systematic assessment of delirium and reversible causes including pain and urinary problems.4,5

Extrapolation
Although urinary retention is not a specific cause of delirium, it may contribute to agitation, distress, or worsening confusion in frail patients.

Interpretation
The tool screens for pain and delirium to provide context but does not block bladder assessment if retention risk is present.
