Low Output Heart Failure Tool — Governance, Evidence, and Methods

Governance support document for the interactive bedside decision-support tool. Version 1.1.
Scope and status
This document explains the reasoning structure, evidence base, areas of extrapolation, and confidence statements for the Low Output Heart Failure – Unfolding Assessment & Treatment Logic tool. It is intended for governance review and local adaptation. The tool supports structured bedside reasoning; it does not replace clinical judgement, local escalation pathways, echocardiography, or specialist review.
Method used for this governance document
References are numbered in order of first appearance and listed at the end in the same sequence. The document preferentially uses major guidelines, society position statements, and primary or peer-reviewed review literature. The structure below follows: Evidence → Extrapolation → Interpretation → Confidence.

1. Tool purpose and intended use

Evidence
Acute heart failure guidance supports structured assessment of symptoms, signs, ECG, imaging, natriuretic peptides, congestion, and perfusion in adults presenting with suspected acute heart failure.1,2 Contemporary heart failure guidance also supports safety-led handling of disease-modifying therapy and decongestion in hospitalised patients.1,3,4
Extrapolation
No major guideline provides this exact multi-module ward tool in the same format. The present tool therefore converts recognised bedside principles into a practical sequence for inpatient use.
Interpretation
The tool is intended for hospital clinicians assessing possible low-output heart failure physiology, especially where multimorbidity, renal dysfunction, diuretic-response uncertainty, frailty, or treatment-escalation questions complicate care.
Confidence
High for the overall clinical domains included. Moderate for the exact sequencing and wording, which are design choices rather than guideline text.

2. Haemodynamic framework: perfusion and congestion as separate axes

Evidence
The bedside “wet/dry” and “warm/cold” framework is embedded in major heart failure guidance and remains a standard conceptual approach to acute heart failure phenotyping.1,3 NICE acute heart failure guidance also supports structured assessment rather than undifferentiated treatment of all breathless or hypotensive patients as though they have the same physiology.2
Extrapolation
The tool uses a simplified two-axis ward interface rather than a full haemodynamic model. It deliberately avoids invasive haemodynamic targets or advanced shock-state modelling.
Interpretation
Separating congestion from perfusion is clinically useful because it prevents reflex treatment based on a single feature alone. It also helps identify higher-risk physiology such as congestion with hypoperfusion, while leaving room for competing explanations such as sepsis, arrhythmia, bleeding, or mixed shock states.
Confidence
High for the conceptual framework. Moderate for individual bedside signs, which are imperfect and context-dependent.

3. Congestion with hypoperfusion: why the tool avoids over-anchoring on heart failure alone

Evidence
Acute heart failure guidelines emphasise that congestion is common but not specific, and that low output, hypotension, renal dysfunction, or lactate rise require rapid reassessment and escalation when instability is present.1,2 The tool’s note that IV loop diuretics are first-line for acute congestion is consistent with guideline and society-position recommendations, while the lack of firm superiority of infusion over bolus reflects trial evidence from the DOSE study.1,3,5
Extrapolation
The tool uses the phrase “begin cautious IV loop diuretic therapy” in congestion with hypoperfusion. This is not a blanket instruction to diurese every hypotensive patient; it is intentionally paired with the warning that septic or mixed shock may mimic this pattern.
Interpretation
The governance intent is to prevent two opposite errors: first, failing to treat clinically important congestion because the blood pressure is low; and second, assuming all “cold and wet” physiology is purely cardiogenic. The wording therefore deliberately links this profile to Section 3 modifiers and Section 6 escalation.
Confidence
Moderate–high. High that congestion and hypoperfusion together are higher risk; moderate regarding the exact bedside action sequence because management depends on the competing diagnosis and senior review.

4. AKI: “wet” versus “dry” interpretation

Evidence
Renal dysfunction in acute heart failure often reflects venous congestion, altered perfusion, neurohormonal activation, and treatment effects rather than a single mechanism.6 Reviews on creatinine changes during decongestion note that small rises in creatinine may occur during effective decongestion and do not necessarily indicate structural kidney injury; values around 0.5 mg/dL (about 45 μmol/L) are often cited as a pragmatic but non-absolute reference point.7
Extrapolation
“Wet AKI” and “dry AKI” are used here as pragmatic bedside categories rather than formally validated diagnostic entities.
Interpretation
The purpose of this module is to reduce reflex stopping of decongestion whenever creatinine rises, while also reducing the opposite error of continuing diuretics blindly in depletion, obstruction, intrinsic renal disease, or non-cardiac AKI. The tool therefore prompts review of bladder status, nephrotoxins, urinary retention, and obstructive uropathy when the picture is non-congestive or uncertain.
Confidence
Moderate–high. High that congestion is important in cardiorenal physiology; moderate for the bedside classification language and any fixed creatinine threshold.

5. Loop diuretic response and urine sodium

Evidence
The HFA/ESC diuretic position statement and related contemporary literature support early natriuresis assessment using spot urine sodium as an objective marker of loop-diuretic responsiveness in acute heart failure.3,8 Recent literature suggests that urine sodium-guided strategies improve early natriuresis and decongestion signals, but consistent hard outcome benefit remains uncertain.8 The tool’s statement that bolus and infusion strategies require different practical interpretation is also aligned with the DOSE trial literature, which did not show a clear overall superiority of continuous infusion over bolus for the primary endpoints.5
Extrapolation
The specific bands used in the tool (>50–70, 30–50, and <30 mmol/L) are pragmatic operational ranges. They are not presented as absolute physiological cut-offs and should not be treated as diagnostic certainties.
Interpretation
This module is intended to make diuretic response more objective without pretending that urine sodium alone determines management. For governance purposes, the text explicitly states that thresholds are approximate and should be integrated with urine output, weight, symptoms, and the overall clinical trajectory.
Confidence
Moderate. High that urine sodium can inform loop-diuretic response assessment; moderate for the exact bracketing and for any implication of outcome improvement.

6. Medication de-escalation versus recommencement/re-escalation

Evidence
Major guidelines support evidence-based heart failure pharmacotherapy in chronic care, but also recognise that hypotension, shock, AKI, hyperkalaemia, bradycardia, or poor intake may necessitate temporary adjustment during acute instability.1,4,9 NICE chronic heart failure guidance and the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline both support structured use of ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta-blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors when clinically appropriate and tolerated.4,9
Extrapolation
The split into Module 5A (de-escalation / temporary withholding) and Module 5B (recommencement and re-escalation) is a design decision rather than a guideline-defined module structure.
Interpretation
This split was deliberately adopted because holding unstable therapy and reintroducing therapy after stabilisation are clinically different tasks. It reduces ambiguity, improves safety language, and better mirrors real inpatient decision-making.
Confidence
High for the need to separate instability-related holding from later reintroduction. Moderate for some of the detailed class-by-class ward wording, which remains judgement-based.

7. Escalation triggers and local pathway linkage

Evidence
NICE acute heart failure guidance supports urgent specialist review and escalation where physiological instability is present.2 Treatment-escalation planning literature in older adults emphasises the importance of shared decision-making, iterative review, and avoiding purely clinician-dominated escalation decisions where the patient has capacity and time allows.10
Extrapolation
The tool explicitly instructs activation of local sepsis, shock, or critical-care pathways where criteria are met. This is a governance choice to ensure the tool sits underneath, rather than above, local emergency pathways.
Interpretation
The escalation wording was intentionally changed from an ICU-centric frame to: “Escalate appropriately and review ceilings of care.” This is more applicable to frail older patients while still supporting timely senior review and higher-dependency escalation where clinically indicated.
Confidence
High. The principle that instability requires prompt escalation is robust; the exact local escalation route remains trust-specific.

8. Cardiology / heart failure specialist prompts

Evidence
Major guideline documents support specialist input where phenotype remains unclear, valvular disease complicates management, arrhythmia is a major driver, or treatment response is inadequate.1,2,4
Extrapolation
The module lists practical prompts rather than strict referral criteria.
Interpretation
The purpose is not to outsource routine ward reasoning but to reduce drift in refractory or uncertain cases.
Confidence
High.

9. Frailty, reversibility, and proportional care

Evidence
Frailty is common in heart failure and is associated with worse outcomes, higher hospitalisation risk, and more complex management needs.11,12 Practical frailty-management papers emphasise that frailty assessment should inform care planning and optimisation rather than act as a blanket exclusion from treatment.11 Shared decision-making literature on treatment escalation planning in older adults reinforces the need to discuss benefits, burdens, reversibility, and patient goals collaboratively.10
Extrapolation
Positioning the frailty module later in the pathway is a design safeguard, chosen to reduce premature therapeutic nihilism.
Interpretation
The governance position is that frailty should shape proportionality of escalation and shared decision-making, not act as an early stop-signal before initial stabilisation and diagnostic clarification.
Confidence
Moderate–high. High that frailty matters; moderate regarding the best exact workflow position, which is partly a safety-oriented design judgement.

10. Diuretic resistance, sodium review, and fluid restriction review

Evidence
The HFA/ESC diuretic statement recognises inadequate response to loop diuretics and supports structured strategy adjustment rather than simple repetition of failing regimens.3 Guideline and review literature also support the importance of sodium balance in congestion and caution against simplistic or ritualised assumptions about fluid restriction in all patients with heart failure.1,2,3
Extrapolation
Splitting “sodium intake review” from “fluid restriction review” is a deliberate design choice to match bedside reality and avoid conflating two different management questions.
Interpretation
This separation reflects common ward practice problems: hidden sodium burden may sustain congestion, whereas reflex 1.5 L restriction may add little benefit in an older patient whose intake is already low.
Confidence
Moderate. High that sodium balance matters; moderate for the exact ward-facing structure and wording.

11. Limitations

Key limitations
  • This is a decision-support framework, not a diagnostic gold standard for low-output heart failure.
  • The tool does not replace echocardiography, ECG interpretation, sepsis pathways, ACS pathways, or senior clinical review.
  • Urine sodium thresholds are pragmatic and approximate.
  • “Wet AKI” and “dry AKI” are useful clinical constructs but are not formally validated diagnostic categories.
  • Frailty language is intended to support proportionality and shared decisions, not denial of treatment.
  • Local policies on AKI, sepsis, drug dosing, treatment escalation planning, and specialist referral should take precedence where they differ.
